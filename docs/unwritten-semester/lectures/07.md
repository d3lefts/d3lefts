## The operating principle

Systems decisions are choices made with incomplete information and limited resources. Reporting expected performance without *uncertainty about the model itself* often overstates what can be optimized. A decision analysis separates (a) uncertain world states, (b) observations one can buy, (c) actions, and (d) losses.

The most useful question is not “Can we improve the model?” It is “Will the information change a decision enough to justify its acquisition cost?” This is the language of **value of information**.

## A two-action decision table

Consider alarm or no alarm, given an observed measurement `y` and an unknown anomaly state `e`. Suppose false alerts cost `C_FP` and missed anomalies cost `C_FN`. With posterior probability `p = P(e=1 | y)`:

```text
Expected loss(alarm | y)    = (1 - p) * C_FP
Expected loss(no alarm | y) = p * C_FN
Alarm when p > C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)
```

This threshold depends on the **loss model** and the **posterior**, neither of which is established by a raw channel count. AURORA's `any` and `quorum` policies are teaching rules, not Bayes-optimal policies.

## Adversarial check

Check the units and who bears each cost. Challenge subjective priors and utilities. If evidence costs money but never changes the optimal action in the model, its decision value is zero even if it reduces prediction uncertainty.

## Doctoral extension

Formulate a sequential sensing policy: choose a sensor conditioned on already observed evidence, stop when additional observations have nonpositive expected net value, and characterize when greedy selection is or is not optimal.
